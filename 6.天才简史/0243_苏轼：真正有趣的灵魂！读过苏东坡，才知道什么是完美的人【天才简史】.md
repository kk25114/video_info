## 简介

这段视频文稿深入探讨了宋代文学家苏轼（苏东坡）的生平、才华及其对后世的影响。文稿首先通过余光中的引言引出苏轼作为一个旅行伴侣的吸引力，随后详细介绍了苏轼在文学、艺术和政治上的卓越成就，以及他在面对政治挫折时的乐观态度和对民生的深切关怀。文稿还讲述了苏轼在黄州贬谪期间的创作和生活，以及他如何将道家的自由精神与儒家的仁爱之心结合，创造出独特的人生哲学。最后，文稿强调了苏轼作为历史上最伟大人物之一的地位，以及他对后世的文化和道德影响。

Before the show starts,  

I would like to ask you a question.  

If you were asked to choose an ancient poet as your traveling friend, who would you choose? Uh, uh, everyone, please don’t try to think of female poets.  

Listen to what Mr. Yu Guangzhong said:  

"Yes, I don’t want to be with Li Bai. He is irresponsible and has no sense of reality. With this tofu, he is too bitter. Haha, but Su Dongpo is good. He can be a very good friend. He is really an interesting person."  

Su Shi, people will be blinded by his brilliance of talent first. Indeed, he is a rare genius once in a millennium, an all-round genius. Poetry, prose, calligraphy, and painting have all reached the pinnacle. With his own efforts, he elevated Song Ci to a height comparable to Tang poetry.  

Song Shenzong commented on Su Shi:  

"Tell Li Bai that Li Bai has Su Shi's talent but not Su Shi's knowledge." Although this is a bit biased—after all, he and the Queen Mother are both Su Shi's fans—Shenzong also has his own basis.  

Let me tell you, then you can see for yourself. Su Shi achieved a third-grade result in the Zhike exam in the sixth year of his family. Its procedures are complicated, and the contents are numerous. It is extremely comprehensive in assessing the academic requirements of the examinees. The grades are divided into five levels: first grade and second grade. Of the 118 champions, only 41 passed the Zhike exam, and only 4 of them were third-class Zhike. The third class was further divided into two grades. Su Shi is the top grade.  

You can imagine, looking at the papers of Su Shi and Su Zhe, Song Renzong said excitedly:  

"I will leave to future generations the talents of two prime ministers."  

But behind Su Shi's gloomy talents, there is no literati's nobility and arrogance. He is a humorous, optimistic, open-minded, kind, and interesting person. Everyone loves him. What is more valuable is that this is not just a good attitude he maintains when life is smooth, but his true nature and feelings that remain unchanged even in the abyss of unforeseen circumstances.  

Virtuous people, well-being, imperial prosperity, and the comprehensive flourishing of economy, technology, and culture—but there are three great policies: honorable soldiers, honorable officials, and honorable concubines. Drawing lessons from the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms, the Song Dynasty became famous.  

However, the policy of supporting soldiers to appease the military guards increasingly burdened the people, resulting in an empty treasury and tight finances. Therefore, after Renzong and Yingzong, Song Shenzong was determined to reform and strengthen the nation, and he reused Wang Anshi to implement the new laws.  

Yet, due to haste, there were many drawbacks. For example, prohibiting the sale of private goods led to official strictness and high costs. The people had no choice but to take risks. In Hangzhou Prefecture alone, 25,000 people were imprisoned for private trading. They were beaten severely, regardless of their suffering.  

Because the conservatives were abolished too quickly, many immoral people supported the new law under false pretenses. Many thought Su Shi opposed the new law, and the quality of officials plummeted. In fact, this was a misunderstanding. Of course, Su Shi supported the positive aspects of the new law. He merely emphasized that reforms should not be too hasty and should proceed step by step.  

But this also angered Shenzong, as he and Wang Anshi were extremely determined and firm in their reforms, comparable to Xiaogong and Shang Yang. The new law was to be implemented, not debated.  

Seeing the court in danger, Su Shi requested a transfer to a local post. On one hand, he could avoid the disaster of partisan disputes; on the other, he could do practical things for the people.  

He repaired wells in Hangzhou, caught locusts in Mizhou, fought floods in Xuzhou, and solved the difficulties of the common people, benefiting the region. Yet, his upright spirit also angered the petty officials in the court. They combed through his poetry and found lines they claimed satirized the emperor, framing and slandering him.  

Under this pressure, Shenzong was furious, and even considered executing Su Shi. This is the famous "Wutai Poetry Case" in history. If not for the pleas of Empress Dowager Cao and Wang Anshi, his life would have been lost.  

It should be noted that although Wang Anshi and Su Shi were not close, Wang was also a noble gentleman. The villains were only those petty officials in the court.  

The death penalty was avoided, but Su Shi could not escape punishment. Stripped of his salary, he had to fend for himself and was confined to Huangzhou.  

At first, Su Shi was deeply depressed. Beyond the frustration of his career, his entire family faced starvation. This was a dire problem.  

So, he rented a piece of military land on a hillside in Chengdu, reclaiming wasteland to plant crops. This is how he earned the name "Dongpo."  

Affordable and easygoing, this was his true nature. From a top-ranking official with millions of admirers, he suddenly became a foodie. The Su family did not complain but mingled with the common people, researching farming techniques, chatting about life, and even sharing ghost stories, laughing every day and enjoying themselves.  

The Wutai Poetry Case and his demotion to Huangzhou were heavy blows, but they also matured him, sublimating his thoughts and emotions. His most important works emerged from this period.  

Even years later, after greater setbacks, Su Shi could still look back on his time in Huangzhou with cheer. Seeing the world's suffering, Huangzhou became a healing place for his soul.  

Notably, though demoted to the status of a commoner, Su Shi still worked tirelessly for the people. Near Huangzhou, poverty often forced families to kill infant girls. Su Shi was heartbroken upon hearing this.  

Though powerless, he leveraged his fame to establish charitable organizations, investigate impoverished rural areas, and save thousands of babies. To address the root cause, he petitioned the local governor to ban infanticide.  

Su Dongpo was selected as one of the 12 greatest figures of the millennium—the only Asian on the list.  

In his eyes, love and kindness were paramount, making life truly worthwhile.  

Some say Su Shi's optimism in Huangzhou was merely masking hardship. This is incorrect. Su Shi had always been a free spirit, even admitting he learned writing techniques from Zhuangzi.  

Zhuangzi, the ultimate joker, saw no hierarchy in the world—everything was just human-imposed concepts. To Su Shi, accompanying the Jade Emperor or a beggar made no difference; they were equal.  

Being demoted to Huangzhou was merely a change of scenery. Whether it was good or bad depended on perspective. Everything in the world has beauty; appreciating it delicately is the happiest way to live.  

For example, upon arriving in Huangzhou, Su Shi quickly discovered its beauty: pork was cheap, but the rich wouldn’t eat it, and the poor didn’t know how to cook it. Thus, "Dongpo Pork" was born.  

Another time, a monk served him overcooked noodles. Su Shi found them crispy, and "Dongpo Cake" was invented.  

Once, during heavy rain, Su Shi walked without an umbrella, soaking every pore. To him, it was fascinating.  

Another night, after drinking with friends, he returned to find his servant asleep. Unbothered, he lay by the river, savoring the tranquility of dawn.  

This spiritual richness is why Yu Guangzhong called him "a person who can make everything interesting."  

What’s commendable is that Su Shi combined Zhuangzi’s free-spirited Buddhism with Confucian benevolence. No matter the circumstances, he never forgot to serve the people.  

This perfect blend of virtues created Su Shi’s adorable, radical, and flawless personality. His stature far surpassed other cynical or escapist literati.  

Beyond his free-spiritedness, Su Shi was also deeply humorous. For example, when his friend Ma Mengde visited him in Huangzhou, both were poor. Su Shi joked:  

"Mengde and I were born in the same year and month, but he’s 8 days younger. People born this year are all poor, and Brother Ma and I are the fighters among the poor."  

He also joked about being a Capricorn, blaming his misfortunes on the zodiac.  

Despite his literary fame, Su Shi was never wealthy. Once, he wrote in his diary:  

"Recently, thieves have come two nights in a row. You want to borrow money? I have none. If you steal a plank, know that my calligraphy is valuable. In 2018, my 'Wood and Rock' sold for 463.6 million Hong Kong dollars."  

Su Shi also had a playful side. Once, he ate meat while chanting Buddhist scriptures. When the monk objected, he rinsed his mouth and continued, leaving the monk speechless.  

When suffering from pink eye, the doctor told him to avoid greasy food, especially meat. Su Shi replied:  

"My brain obeys, but my mouth doesn’t."  

His friend Foyin often teased him. Once, Su Shi wrote a Zen poem:  

"The rooster crows, the sky brightens; the wind cannot move me. Sitting upright on a golden lotus."  

Proud, he sent it to Foyin, who replied: "Fart."  

Furious, Su Shi crossed the river to confront him. Foyin smiled:  

"You claim the wind can’t move you, yet one 'fart' made you cross the river?"  

Su Shi realized his restlessness and laughed.  

Later, Foyin told him to meditate. Bored, Su Shi asked:  

"What do I look like?"  

Foyin: "A Buddha."  

Su Shi, pleased, asked: "And what do you look like?"  

Foyin remained silent. Su Shi said: "Like shit."  

Foyin smirked.  

Upon reflection, Su Shi understood: "If I see Buddha in you, my heart is Buddha. If I see shit, my heart is shit."  

Despite his Buddhist and Taoist leanings, Su Shi’s true foundation was Confucianism—serving the people.  

In 1085, the 38-year-old Song Shenzong died. Song Zhezong succeeded, with Empress Dowager Gao regent. Reformists were suppressed, and conservatives regained power.  

Sima Guang, whom Su Shi admired, was recalled. In just 17 months, Su Shi rose from a lowly criminal to a third-rank official.  

But trouble arose when Sima Guang sought to completely overturn the reforms. Su Shi, having witnessed the reforms' benefits, opposed this. He suggested retaining the good parts and modifying the rest.  

This angered Sima Guang, leaving Su Shi ostracized by both factions.  

Refusing to compromise, Su Shi chose to serve locally. He was appointed prefect of Hangzhou, where he revitalized West Lake, building the famous "Su Causeway" with dredged silt.  

He also innovated by leasing the lake for water chestnut farming, preventing aquatic weeds while boosting the economy.  

To mark boundaries, he built three stone pagodas, now the iconic "Three Pools Mirroring the Moon."  

Beyond art, Su Shi’s greatest legacy was his民生 work. He established China’s first public hospital, "Anlefang," during a plague, even selling his wife’s jewelry to fund it.  

Doctors treated patients for free, delivering medicine weekly—a revolutionary concept.  

In 1093, Empress Dowager Gao died, and Zhezong resumed reforms. Su Shi and 30 others were demoted again.  

Exiled to Huizhou (then a cultural backwater), Su Shi improved local governance, medicine, and agriculture. He even designed bamboo pipes to bring clean water to Guangzhou—China’s first running water system.  

Despite hardships, he remained cheerful, inventing dishes like roasted lamb spine (when mutton was scarce) and praising lychees:  

"300 lychees a day, and I’d gladly stay in Lingnan forever."  

Later, he was exiled further—to Hainan, then a penal colony. At 60, he arrived with his youngest son, Su Guo, and a coffin, ready to die there.  

Yet, he taught farming, dispelled superstitions, and mentored Hainan’s first imperial scholar.  

Discovering oysters, he joked:  

"Don’t tell the court, or they’ll come stealing my oysters."  

In 1100, Huizong’s amnesty recalled him. Ill, he retired but died en route in Changzhou.  

His last words:  

"I’ve done no wrong in life. Don’t cry. There’s no separate Western Paradise—it exists in how we live each day."  

Su Dongpo’s legacy isn’t just art but his love for the people, optimism, and ability to find beauty everywhere.  

He endured suffering to serve others, like a Bodhisattva. True immortals aren’t aloof but walk among us, easing suffering with kindness and wisdom.  

His light still guides us today.
