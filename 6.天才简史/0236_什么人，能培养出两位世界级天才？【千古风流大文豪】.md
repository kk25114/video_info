Before the Show Starts  

I'd like to talk to you about talent.  

As a dynasty, the Song Dynasty attached great importance to culture and education, nurturing a generation of talent. It was truly star-studded: six of the eight major literary families of the Tang and Song dynasties were from the Song Dynasty. Countless thinkers and cultural celebrities emerged, their brilliance shining like gods, their creativity bursting forth. Their poems and essays have become a nightmare for middle school students.  

But there was a curious problem: since the Song Dynasty had so many talents, why was it so weak militarily? The Liao and Western Xia repeatedly bullied it into a complete mess. What was the true role of this abundance of talent in strengthening the country?  

In the Spring and Autumn Period and the Warring States Period, there was no standing army of 100,000 warriors, yet figures like Guan Zhong, Shang Yang, Li Kui, Wu Qi, Zhang Yi, Sun Wu, and others emerged, each capable of reversing the universe and altering the world's landscape, enabling their states to dominate. But such transformative figures seem hard to find in the mid-Song period. The Song Dynasty continued to decline, even shrinking from "Great Song" to "Small Song." Was there something wrong with its concept of talent, or did it truly lack figures like Guan Zhong?  

Han Yu said, "Thousand-li horses are common, but Bole is rare." People remain the same; little has changed over a thousand years. The Song Dynasty also had talents like Guan Zhong, Wu Qi, Zhang Yi, Su Qin, and Shang Yang. But why couldn't they turn things around? This is worth pondering.  

In this episode of *Tang Song Eight*, we delve into the strategic thinker Su Xun's ideas and encounters to dissect this vexing problem.  

First, we need to understand how profound Su Xun's thinking was.  

Su Xun failed three times in the imperial examinations before abandoning them for good and focusing on saving the country, reversing its fortunes through study. He stopped chasing fame, immersed himself in history, and rigorously analyzed knowledge. Despite his insights, he refrained from drawing hasty conclusions or blindly following others. Instead, he deliberated and debated repeatedly, refining his understanding until his mind was tempered. Only then did he put pen to paper.  

When his articles emerged, they were like a tumultuous sea, vast and profound, breathtaking in their majesty. So, what were Su Xun's key ideas?  

First, Su Xun believed history should not be glorified but learned from. It should be studied with a realistic attitude—no one is perfect. We should recognize our predecessors' merits but also their flaws, even their misdeeds, and analyze them. What were the ancients' virtues? What were their evils? Were they minor or major? How did these evils arise? Were there underlying causes? What connections existed between good and evil? These lessons must be understood to solve real problems.  

For example, in the 6th year of the Jiayou era (1061), while studying the *Book of Rites* with Yao Bi, he criticized the editing style of the time. Editors sought to whitewash history, erasing ancestors' faults as if mowing grass without leaving a trace. Su Xun pointed out that writing history is not art; subjective biases should not distort facts. If only the good is recorded and the bad omitted, historical precedents become chaotic and contradictory. Future generations would be left puzzled, with nothing to learn from.  

During the rise of Neo-Confucianism, courtiers debated state affairs, quoting the sages endlessly. But their citations were often from the *Spring and Autumn Annals*, which had been heavily edited. They praised the past without considering context, severing cause and effect, ignoring historical limitations. This was like carving a prisoner's confession to fit a narrative—utterly worthless. Unsurprisingly, policies implemented this way failed spectacularly.  

Su Xun, free from such constraints, viewed history with his own insight, clarifying cause and effect as if witnessing the truth. His ideas were far more practical.  

For example, Liu Bang's refusal to curb Empress Lü's power in his later years or kill Fan Kuai was once seen as senility. But Su Xun offered a profound take: this was Liu Bang's wisdom. Empress Lü had fought alongside him and commanded high prestige among courtiers. By leaving her intact, Liu Bang avoided posthumous chaos like the unrest after King Wu of Zhou's death. It was a calculated move to protect Emperor Hui of Han.  

Yet, the Lü clan still had to be checked. So, Liu Bang appointed Zhou Bo as commander to counter them after his death. In Liu Bang's view, the Lü clan was manageable, but Fan Kuai—Empress Lü's brother-in-law and a formidable warrior—was uncontrollable. That was the real reason Liu Bang wanted him dead.  

In evaluating Guan Zhong, Su Xun noted that while Guan Zhong was a capable chancellor, he failed to curb the corruption of his subordinates during his lifetime, only warning the king on his deathbed. This was dereliction. Guan Zhong's biggest flaw was not grooming a qualified successor, leaving the state vulnerable.  

Su Xun's insights on talent management were profoundly instructive. He excelled not only at analyzing historical figures but also at critiquing contemporary leaders. For instance, in a letter to Chancellor Fu Bi, he recounted how the Duke of Zhou, while regent, tolerated Shao Gong's suspicions until quelling three rebellions before addressing them. Su Xun urged Fu Bi to cultivate tolerance, even for petty men, and focus on principal contradictions.  

This lesson was lost on Fan Zhongyan, a staunch supporter of the Qingli Reforms, who clashed endlessly with petty men, leading to the reform's failure. Su Xun's teachings deeply influenced his sons, Su Shi and Su Zhe. He encouraged them to study historical precedents for modern problems, learning from past mistakes.  

Su Xun was not just politically astute; his strategic and military insights were equally sharp. He even boasted that his *Strategies of War* surpassed ancient texts. He likened himself to Jia Yi of the Western Han, confident in his military acumen.  

Since its founding, the Northern Song had faced relentless threats, bribing the Liao for peace. Su Xun's *On the Six States* warned that the six states fell precisely because they ceded land to Qin. If the Song followed suit, its fate would be worse. Tragically, his prophecy came true 60–70 years later.  

Su Xun urged rulers to abandon illusions and confront the Khitan actively. Bribing an enemy bent on conquest only delayed the inevitable. He also analyzed Sun Wu and Wu Qi's tactics, offering unique insights on espionage and logistics.  

But his ideas clashed with Neo-Confucian orthodoxy. Scholars dismissed him as a throwback to the Warring States era, unworthy of a gentleman. Yet, Su Xun argued that figures like Su Qin and Zhang Yi succeeded precisely because they used history as a guide, making their arguments irrefutable.  

Despite Neo-Confucian disdain, Su Xun's works gained acclaim and influenced many. His rise to fame is a tale of perseverance. After abandoning exams, he studied in obscurity until discovered by Zhang Fangping, a prodigy with a photographic memory. Zhang, struck by Su Xun's brilliance, treated him as an equal, even reserving a seat in his home solely for him.  

Zhang recommended Su Xun as an education official, but Lei Jinfu, another admirer, deemed this inadequate. Lei compared Su Xun to a kingmaker and pressed Prime Minister Han Qi and Ouyang Xiu to recommend him. Ouyang Xiu, upon reading Su Xun's works, praised them as akin to Xunzi's—a monumental compliment.  

Yet, despite literary fame and his sons' exam success, Su Xun's appointment stalled. Chancellor Fu Bi, slighted by Su Xun's critiques, sidelined him. When his wife died, Su Xun returned home, only to be summoned later for an exam. He refused, citing age and dignity—he would not endure the humiliation of testing like a youth.  

This reflected the Song rulers' flawed approach to talent. Unlike Qin Shihuang, who elevated men like Li Si (a petty clerk) and Yao Jia (a bandit) without exams, the Song fixated on formalities. At 51, Su Xun was offered a minor post, his genius wasted.  

The Song Dynasty brimmed with talent, but its rulers lacked vision. They sought to preserve, not expand, their realm, blind to their precarious position. This was the tragedy of Su Xun—and of the Song itself.
