# 这个英国人，半辈子都在为中国历史正名！【天才简史】

**原始链接:** <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ghqYQ0tdDIw>

## 简介

这段视频文稿讲述了英国生物化学家约瑟夫·尼达姆（Joseph Needham）如何因为一次与中国学生卢桂珍的对话，改变了对中国科技成就的看法，并最终将其一生奉献于研究和传播中国古代科学技术的故事。从最初的质疑到深入了解，尼达姆被中国古代的科技成就和中国人民的坚韧不拔所感动，他不仅在中国抗日战争期间提供了大量援助，还撰写了多卷本的《中国科学技术史》，改变了西方对中国古代科技的认识。尼达姆的故事是对文化交流和相互理解的深刻诠释，展现了他对中国文化和科学技术的深厚热爱和尊重。

Miss Lu, why is China so good at scientific discovery?  

So little has been achieved.  

At the first dinner table in Cambridge, England,  
a heroic English professor  
asked one of his Chinese students.  

The professor  
was not attempting to humiliate Miss Lu from China.  
In the 1930s or so,  
the general view of British scholars on Chinese technology  
was always like that.  

"Excuse me, professor,  
how many Chinese books have you read?  
How much do you know about China?  
On what basis are you sure  
China has had little success in scientific invention?"  

In ancient China,  
many achievements in science and culture  
far surpassed those in the West.  
And some inventions,  
such as paper and gunpowder,  
to this day have played an important role in world history.  

Lu Guizhen, a student from China,  
whom the English professor just called Miss Lu,  
said this.  
Judging by the tone of voice,  
she did get a little angry.  

The British professor sitting opposite realized this too.  
He did not think his own seemingly ordinary question  
would anger his Chinese student.  
After listening to Lu Guizhen speak about ancient China's  
scientific and technological achievements,  
he knew where he stood.  
That was a lot to take in.  
He quickly apologized to Lu Guizhen.  

The English professor was Joseph Needham.  
But we later generations  
usually prefer to call him by another name:  
Joseph Needham.  

His original research field was biochemical embryology,  
and the conversation he just had with Lu Guizhen  
opened a new window for his dreary laboratory.  
Since then, he has had an indissoluble bond with China.  
In Needham's words,  
this conversation changed the course of his life.  

Needham followed Nobel laureate  
Hopkins in studying biochemistry  
and made no small achievements in scientific research.  
At a young age,  
he was named senior lecturer at Cambridge University.  
At 31,  
he published *Residential Chemistry*, which established him as an academic.  
Embryology established a whole discipline system.  
The publication of this book  
made Needham famous at Cambridge University.  

If this professor had spent the rest of his life  
dedicated to biochemistry,  
he might have won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry  
or Physiology or Medicine.  
But no one would have expected  
that at the height of his scientific career,  
he would take a different path.  

After the conversation we just mentioned,  
Needham began to immerse himself in the study of Chinese.  
He was pleasantly surprised  
to find that a single Chinese character  
contained a wealth of culture.  
He was determined to learn the language well.  
Between his daily biological studies,  
learning Chinese  
became Needham's greatest passion.  

In the process,  
Needham delved into  
the scientific and technological achievements of ancient China.  
Before completely changing his  
perception of China as technologically backward,  
he discovered that Chinese calligraphy  
was also regarded as a kind of artistic accomplishment.  
He immersed himself in the study of Chinese calligraphy.  
Through the strokes of point, skimming, and捺,  
Joseph Needham  
for the first time felt the profound culture of the ancient Eastern civilization.  
He was deeply moved by China,  
a country that had once seemed mysterious.  

He wrote in his diary: "I fell in love with China,  
fell in love with the entire Chinese civilization."  

In 1937, the Japanese army launched  
the Nanjing Massacre.  
Needham was furious when he heard the news.  
Nanjing was the hometown of his student and teacher Lu Guizhen.  
As a lover of Chinese culture,  
he could hardly contain his anger.  
He deeply disliked the British government's  
neutral attitude toward the Japanese invasion of China.  
He marched on London when he got the chance,  
holding signs written in Chinese:  
"Don't buy Japanese goods for Christmas."  
He also wrote letters to newspapers  
to call attention to China's plight  
and to rally support for China.  

The invading Japanese army tried to wipe out Chinese culture,  
dispatching aircraft multiple times  
to target Chinese colleges and universities.  
This forced many of China's leading universities  
to relocate inland.  

In this time of crisis,  
a group of English people organized  
to assist China's struggling colleges.  
When Needham heard the news,  
he immediately joined the Chinese aid team.  

Joseph Needham at the time  
was in the prime of his scientific career.  
Upon learning of his plans to go to China,  
many professors and students at Cambridge University  
tried to dissuade him.  
"You're a highly respected biology professor.  
Why give up the comforts of Cambridge  
for a country at war?"  
None of this persuaded Needham.  
He still lived his life  
with no regrets.  

At the end of 1942,  
Needham slowly descended the steps of the plane.  
In his blue eyes  
was the China he had loved for so many years.  
But the view before him  
was slightly different from what he had expected.  
That glorious and powerful country  
was now shattered by war.  
The sight filled his heart with grief.  

Yet in this cruel environment,  
Needham also discovered something surprising.  
Here, old carts irrigated the land,  
nourishing the barren soil with new hope.  
A flexible abacus  
maintained fair trade in the hands of shopkeepers.  
Solid stone arch bridges  
stood firm against the flow of rivers.  
Things he had only read about in books  
now came alive before his eyes.  

With a closer look at these ancient tools,  
Needham grew to admire the wisdom of the ancient Chinese.  
He then went to Southwest Associated University in Kunming.  
Kunming, known as the City of Sunshine,  
now had no daylight in the sky—  
only the looming threat of air raids.  
Upon visiting the school,  
he found there wasn't even a bomb shelter.  
Additionally,  
he noticed buckets in the classrooms.  
At first, he didn't know their purpose,  
only to learn that when students heard the air raid alarm,  
they would first place precious test equipment  
into these buckets  
to protect them from bomb damage.  

Needham was shocked  
and deeply moved by the Chinese students'  
dedication to learning.  

When visiting Tongji University in Lizhuang,  
Needham met his old friend,  
biologist P.C. Tang.  
Tang, who had studied abroad,  
was once known to Needham for his excellent surgical skills.  
Upon seeing Tang's experimental environment in China,  
Needham was stunned.  
On an old wooden table,  
a few simple beakers were laid out.  
The only light source at night  
was a dim, pungent kerosene lamp.  
The only microscope on the table  
had been bought by Tang with borrowed money from a street stall.  
Wages in the Red Basin states were pitiful;  
their two years' salary combined  
wasn't enough to cover the price of the microscope.  

Yet in this humble environment,  
Tang's lab achieved results  
on par with the best in Europe.  
Needham was astonished.  
He asked Tang why, with such opportunities in Europe,  
he had returned to this remote mountain village  
to conduct experiments.  
Tang replied with a smile,  
"Because I am Chinese."  

In Needham's diary,  
there is a passage like this:  
"The Chinese have a democratic sense of humor  
and the lofty social ideals of Confucianism.  
To think the Chinese would yield to  
the right flank of Japanese imperialist aggressors  
is impossible."  

The unyielding quality of Chinese scholars  
deeply moved Needham.  
He became thoroughly infatuated with China—  
its scenery and its people.  
He also spontaneously wanted to do something  
for this country in distress.  

Wherever Needham went,  
in addition to studying local science and technology  
and the broader culture,  
he also tried to address the problems  
of Chinese scientists lacking books and equipment.  

Many Chinese at the time  
were unaware of their country's  
once-brilliant scientific and technological achievements.  
To remedy this,  
Needham gave hundreds of speeches across China.  
His speeches had only one theme:  
China's major contributions to world science and technology.  

In his colorful speeches,  
people learned about ancient China's compass,  
papermaking, and other achievements  
that had a major impact on the world.  
They also discovered that vaccines developed by China in recent years  
and radio products had been adopted in Britain and America,  
playing important roles in World War II.  

In 1945, China won the War of Resistance against Japan.  
Joseph Needham  
would also return to England with the British team.  
Over three years,  
Joseph Needham  
donated more than 6,000 books to China  
and over £60,000 worth of experimental equipment.  
The sheer scale is mind-boggling.  
It's hard to imagine a foreigner  
willing to expend so much energy  
to advance science in China.  

He founded the Sino-British Science Cooperation Office in Chongqing,  
through which 138 papers by domestic scholars  
were published in foreign journals,  
allowing their academic achievements to reach the world.  

By this point,  
Needham had an even grander plan in mind.  
During his time in China,  
he had traveled north and south along its great rivers,  
conducting in-depth investigations  
into the country's scientific and technological achievements.  
He saw China's water mills  
and only upon deeper research learned  
that the technology had spread from China to the world.  
He saw carriages in China  
and studied the ancient Chinese methods of horse harnessing,  
only to discover that the Western method  
also originated in China.  
This method greatly improved  
horse efficiency,  
with China leading the West by 500 years.  

He saw sail-powered wheelbarrows in China  
and began studying them with great interest.  
The results showed that  
this technology had been introduced to Europe in ancient times,  
directly contributing to the transportation revolutions  
in Europe and Egypt.  

Ancient China's contributions to the world  
cannot be summarized in a few words.  
But today, the Western understanding of ancient China  
is limited to silk and porcelain,  
with almost no recognition of its technological achievements.  
Needham knew  
that the great mission of making the West aware of ancient Chinese technology  
now rested on his shoulders.  

He abandoned his studies in biochemical embryology  
and devoted the rest of his life's energy  
to writing *Science and Civilization in China*.  
The writing of this book had just begun  
when Cambridge voiced its objections.  
Even among Needham's family and friends,  
there was much opposition to his project.  

"If Needham had continued in biochemistry,  
he would surely have achieved greater academic success—  
perhaps even a Nobel Prize.  
Why should he set aside his established field  
to write about another country's achievements?"  
Others argued that Needham, as a biologist,  
couldn't possibly write about science and technology.  

Amid this cacophony of criticism,  
Needham followed his own wishes.  
He closed his lab door and sat down at the typewriter.  
He initially planned to finish the book in a year,  
but soon realized  
there was too much to summarize.  
So he decided to write an entire series.  

Six years passed in a flash.  
He gave no lectures as a professor  
and conducted no research.  
No one at Cambridge cared anymore  
about Needham's former exalted position.  
Gradually, threats emerged,  
and some even tried to expel him.  
Needham knew his situation,  
but he believed  
what he was doing was meaningful.  

In 1954,  
Needham submitted the first volume of *Science and Civilization in China*  
to the publisher.  
At this moment,  
he wondered how the book would be received.  
If it failed,  
he risked losing his job at Cambridge,  
and all his scientific achievements would be overshadowed.  

To his surprise,  
the book was an instant success.  
The first printing of 5,000 copies sold out immediately,  
and the publisher had to reprint it repeatedly.  
Through this book,  
Europe's perception of ancient China  
was transformed.  
Academic acclaim poured in,  
and Needham was praised for making "ten important contributions."  

After more than a decade of effort,  
Needham finally received recognition.  
In the colorful statistics,  
many "China firsts" were highlighted.  
People were astonished to find  
that China produced about 15 major inventions per century—  
a pace unmatched by any European country.  
Many European inventions  
actually originated from Chinese innovations.  
China's contributions to human science and technology  
had been a pillar of progress.  

In the 1920s,  
China was widely seen in the West as backward and inflexible.  
This bias  
stemmed from racial exclusion  
and cultural self-aggrandizement.  
But when they opened the pages of history  
and looked at China anew,  
they could rediscover the Eastern dragon.  

Needham continued writing  
*Science and Civilization in China*,  
a monumental work spanning seven volumes  
and 30 books, covering all aspects of Chinese  
scientific and technological achievements.  
Needham began at age 48  
and spent the next 48 years—  
nearly half a century—  
building bridges between civilizations.  

Through his incredible perseverance,  
he made the Western world  
re-examine ancient Chinese science and technology.  
Ancient Chinese science  
was thus incorporated into the global  
academic system of the history of science.  

Joseph Needham  
proved with his results that his original choice  
was right.  

On Friday, March 24, 1995,  
the typing fingers of the old man, nearly 95 years old,  
fell still forever.  
The old man spent his whole life  
changing Western attitudes toward the Chinese nation's  
ancient science and technology,  
making an indelible contribution  
to human understanding.  

To this day,  
Cambridge University tells the story of three trees.  
The first is an apple tree,  
under which Newton  
discovered gravity.  
The second is a mulberry tree,  
where poet John Milton  
wrote famous poems like *Christmas Psalm*,  
becoming a poet of English civilization.  
The third is a linden tree,  
an Oriental species  
with a unique style in Cambridge's European landscape.  
Under this tree  
lie the ashes of Joseph Needham.  
And the story behind this tree  
is the one you just heard.
