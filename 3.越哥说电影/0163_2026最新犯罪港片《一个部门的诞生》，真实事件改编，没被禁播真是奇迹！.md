A man charged into a cable company's service center with a gun, holding everyone hostage. He didn't want money or hostages; he just wanted to cancel his TV plan.

"I see, I see."

"You're going too far, really."

"I don't understand. What do you mean?"

"The root of this farce lies entirely with the TV company. To prevent cancellations, they use every trick in the book, with dozens of rules for canceling; if a customer gets even 1 wrong, they have to start the application all over again. The service center is packed every day with customers wanting to cancel, and the low-level customer service agents spend their days dealing with them."

Xiao Mei just started and is already questioning her life after being yelled at by customers. The veteran, Fatty, sees through it all and gives her a toy, teaching her earnestly.

"I..."

"I feel the same."

To Fatty, customer service is just the company's shield; no matter what customers say, just keep smiling, say thank you, and let it go in one ear and out the other—just don't get a complaint.

One day, a young man named De Zai came to the center to cancel his grandmother's TV plan, and Fatty told him to go take a number and wait as usual. Right after De Zai left, Officer Li rushed to the counter. He'd missed his number while in the restroom and asked for a favor, but in this company, they love creating trouble for customers, not solving problems. Fatty cited the company's regulations and told him to get back in line.

Officer Li lost his patience, pulled out his police badge, and warned Fatty that he'd charge him with obstructing official business if he didn't cooperate. Fatty remained unmoved: "I'd love to help, but it's company policy. If I help you, the others will complain. We should respect everyone's status equally."

It was soon time to clock out, and while many customers were still waiting, Fatty immediately dropped his work and prepared to leave. De Zai quickly blocked his way, pulling out the contract and confirmation letter, demanding he cancel the plan immediately. Seeing De Zai wouldn't let up, Fatty took him to the lounge and told him to call the hotline himself. It was clearly a brush-off, and De Zai refused, leading to a heated argument.

Officer Li rushed over, showed his queue number, and made a deal with Fatty: if he processed his business first, he would help get rid of the headache that was De Zai. Unexpectedly, during the scuffle, Officer Li's gun fell to the floor. He didn't think much of it at first and kept provoking De Zai, who, in a fit of rage, snatched the gun away. The farce had spiraled out of control.

Forced into a corner, De Zai decided to go all the way, holding the gun to Fatty's head and demanding a cancellation. But when Fatty checked the system, there was no record of his subscription. It turns out that according to the contract, if a user fails to return the equipment within the specified time, any previous cancellation request is automatically voided. De Zai was furious after hearing this. He had followed all the procedures, yet his request was wiped out just because of a minor technicality.

Enraged, De Zai made everyone hand over their phones and herded them into the break room. To prevent anyone from calling the police, he unplugged the landline and locked the door. A female customer service agent seized the chaos as an opportunity to escape through the back door, and her shouts for help drew the attention of a reporter working nearby. Sensing a big scoop, the reporter sneaked in through the back, only to be caught red-handed by De Zai, who dragged her and a fleeing Fatty back inside.

De Zai insisted repeatedly that he didn't want money; he just wanted to cancel his plan. Only then did Fatty reveal the truth: ordinary customer service reps like them have no authority to cancel plans; it has to be done at headquarters. The company intentionally designed the process to be so complicated that customers would find it troublesome and eventually just give up. De Zai had no choice but to force Xiao Mei to call them at gunpoint. But as soon as the call connected, it wasn't a real person but a long string of ads and voice menus.

Unfamiliar with the system, Xiao Mei didn't know which keys to press. The blue-haired guy, who usually played the company mascot, stepped in to help. He knew the process by heart; he knew exactly what to press before the recording even finished. Even after getting through to a representative, they still had to wait in a long queue.

While De Zai was distracted by the phone, Officer Li planned to team up with the others to seize the gun. Everyone took turns finding excuses to divert De Zai's attention, but just as the officer was about to make a move, the phone rang—it was the police calling. De Zai hung up in a panic.

Seeing his fear, Officer Li seized the moment and pressured him: "Put the gun down, De Zai. You're going to get yourself killed." But De Zai's gaze grew even more determined; even if he were to be arrested, he was going to get that plan canceled. This resolve moved Blue Hair, who was standing nearby, and he decided to join De Zai in standing up against the police. It turned out that he was a council member for the Victims of Predatory TV Alliance, posing as a mascot to rally others scammed by the station.

The back door is blocked by De Zai, and the police don't dare storm in knowing there are hostages. De Zai knows full well that the police will rush in sooner or later. To speed things up, he lets the least threatening hostages out of the lounge to help make phone calls. After trying again and again, the call finally goes through, but that's only the first hurdle. Director Yang, who picks up the phone, is notoriously difficult; he checks information repeatedly, and if you're 1 second late to answer, he just hangs up. Although Blue Hair reminded De Zai to be prepared, they still missed the requirement that the original account holder must be present, and it was De Zai's grandma who bought the package. Director Yang prepares to reject the application based on this alone, but at the critical moment, Fatty steps up and suggests having an old lady nearby pretend to be his grandma, which keeps Yang on the line.

Fatty then takes over, negotiating with Director Yang on De Zai's behalf. Fatty knows the company's procedures inside out and shuts Yang down in a few sentences: "The company can move, people can move, the world can move, do you understand?"

Fatty, who once let Xiao Mei do the opening, doesn't miss this time; Director Yang is swayed and sends over the application form, and De Zai takes the first step toward canceling.

Meanwhile, the police are investigating De Zai's background, but they refuse to believe someone would hold hostages just to cancel a TV subscription. Inspector San-jie of the Major Crimes Unit is convinced there's a massive conspiracy and immediately calls in the S.D.U.

De Zai finishes filling out the form, but Fatty tells him that according to company policy, it will take 2 business days to process. De Zai is certainly not willing to accept that; what if he gets arrested and the station goes back on its word? Just then, Officer Li suddenly lunges at him, trying to grab the gun. During the struggle, the gun goes off accidentally, and the bullet hits Officer Li in the thigh.

De Zai is completely terrified and rushes to have others bandage Officer Li, but before they finish, a foul stench fills the air; everyone realizes Officer Li was so scared that he wet himself, and the police's dignity is wiped out instantly.

"I'm done for."

As soon as the gunshot rings out, the S.D.U. prepares to storm the building. Blue Hair suggested taking initiative rather than just sitting around waiting, so De Zai contacted Inspector San-jie, promising to release the hostages if the police helped him cancel the subscription. To show his sincerity, he released the wounded Officer Li first, and Li informed Inspector San-jie about the situation.

"I think it's ridiculous to reach a point where someone has to use a gun; it's too much."

Inspector San-jie finally realized how difficult this TV station's service plan was to cancel; even as the leader of the Major Crimes Unit, she couldn't handle it. The station only had 1 person in each department, and they kept passing the buck, all claiming that cancellations weren't their responsibility. After finally getting approval, she had to call a technician to come and collect the set-top box.

Meanwhile, De Zai was waiting anxiously until he turned and saw a reporter secretly filming him, causing him to become alert immediately.

"I'm carrying."

The reporter wasn't intimidated and asked the question that had been on her mind: why did De Zai have to cancel the subscription today of all days? She wanted to record De Zai's story and make it public, hoping more people would come forward to support him. These words made De Zai lower his guard and finally share his story.

It turned out that De Zai lost his parents at a young age and was raised by his grandmother. Later, he fell in love with cinema and dreamed of becoming a screenwriter. For the sake of his dream, he was busy working all day and rarely went home; even when he did, he would always argue with his grandmother over trivial matters in life. Once, he discovered his grandmother had signed up for a Happy TV subscription plan, and his first reaction wasn't to ask why, but to blame her for wasting money, leading to a bitter argument.

De Zai didn't go home for 3 months after that, until his grandmother's birthday, when Sister Tang finally found him. Only then did he learn why she had signed up: a salesman from Happy TV told her that the membership package included a movie channel featuring many Hong Kong films. Grandma thought if she subscribed to this channel, she could watch her grandson's movies on TV, but she didn't know the monthly fees would keep rising and that canceling required a series of complex procedures.

Upon hearing this, De Zai finally decided to go home, but when he arrived, he found his grandmother unconscious on the sofa, still tightly clutching the cancellation confirmation notice that never took effect. At that moment, De Zai realized he had ignored his grandmother's feelings all these years. He didn't know how to make amends and just wanted to settle the matter for her, but he never expected that someone like him would face endless refusals—how many doors must have been slammed in his grandmother's face?

After hearing De Zai's story, the reporter finally understood his persistence. She recorded everything and broadcast it live, letting more people know the truth behind the incident.

Just then, the power in the business hall went out. Everyone thought the police were about to storm in, and the atmosphere became tense again. Fortunately, Inspector San-jie arrived in time. She said the police didn't cut the power and that she had already obtained a cancellation confirmation. But De Zai insisted on waiting for the confirmation to be verified before letting anyone go.

Inspector San-jie was no longer as tough as she had been at the start. After going through the cancellation process herself, she finally understood why De Zai was being so extreme. Just then, De Zai's cousin arrived at the scene with his grandma.

Thinking her grandson was robbing a bank, the grandma was so scared she nearly had a heart attack. De Zai had to lie and say he was filming a movie, and the police played along to help him finish the act.

Everyone thought this was unlikely, but to their surprise, De Zai soon received a call from the boss, who offered to exchange himself for the hostages. In reality, he had a plan. He bet that De Zai wouldn't dare to pull the trigger. The "hostage exchange" was just a publicity stunt. By the next day, his act of heroism would be front-page news, his stock price would soar, and the negative impact of the incident would be easily reversed.

This beautiful blueprint swayed De Zai, but just as he was about to put down the gun, the boss suddenly grabbed him around the neck from behind.

"It's okay, it's okay."

"Everyone."

As the two struggled over the gun, it fell to the ground and went off again. The shot hit De Zai directly in the chest. In no time, the news that the TV station boss had driven a customer to his death over a cancellation spread everywhere. Not long after, the TV station was on the verge of bankruptcy due to this malicious incident, and most of the staff were laid off.

Before leaving, Xiao Mei took out the toy that Fatty had given her.

"The Birth of a Department," released in 2026, was directed by Mai Tianshu. He was formerly a screenwriter at Milkyway Image; this was his directorial debut, and the film was nominated for the Asian New Talent Award at the 28th Shanghai International Film Festival. The story wasn't just made up; over a decade ago in Hong Kong, a young man actually broke into a TV station with a knife because he couldn't cancel his television subscription. Back then, it was just a piece of social news that people laughed off. Mai Tianshu turned it into a Hong Kong-style dark comedy—no intense shootouts or complex criminal conspiracies, just an ordinary person pushed to the brink, step by step, all for the sake of canceling a TV package.

To be honest, I was laughing while watching this movie, but eventually, the laughter died out because we have all experienced the hurdles in the film: calling customer service only to listen to a string of ads, waiting forever to reach a human agent, only to be told it's not their department, having to verify your ID all over again, and finally being told you must appear in person. And don't forget those auto-renewing subscriptions—it takes 1 second to sign up, but turning them off requires digging through layers of phone menus.

Fatty put it clearly: the company intentionally designs the process to be this complicated so that customers, finding it too troublesome, will simply give up. That is the most terrifying part of the whole film—the person making things difficult for you isn't some villain, but a set of rules designed for that very purpose. Everyone follows the rules, and everyone says it's not their responsibility, but it is exactly these rules combined that kept an elderly woman trying to cancel her service locked out for months.

The title is "The Birth of a Department," but by the end of the film, you realize the company never really had a department responsible for cancellations; every department only had 1 person, and everyone was just passing the buck. Perhaps the price for the birth of such a department is the cost of a human life.

But what made me feel the most uncomfortable in the film wasn't the protagonist; it was those customer service agents in the office. They get cursed at as idiots and losers by customers every day, yet they themselves are just people trapped by these very same rules. Fatty teaches Xiao Mei to treat herself like a switch—turn off your senses, turn off your emotions—so that when you get yelled at, you won't feel the pain. It sounds heartbreaking, but it is the survival wisdom for many working people.

In this TV station, customers don't treat service agents like people; the company doesn't treat employees like people; and the boss doesn't treat users like people. In his eyes, a $10 million check can buy De Zai's compromise and tomorrow's front-page headline; everyone has a price. He said that so easily because, in his world, people were always meant to have a price tag. That is why what Fatty said to the owner of the dog was so heartbreaking: "The whole world might not treat you like a human, but have you ever treated yourself like one?" That line was less of a message to the owner and more of a reminder to himself. The man who once urged Xiao Mei to turn off her switch finally switched his own back on in that moment.

Of course, the film doesn't portray De Zai as a hero; he is impulsive, willful, and ended up harming a room full of innocent people. Yet, when someone tries to defend their rights through normal channels only to be blocked again and again, extreme measures are all they have left. What's even more tragic is that he had to sacrifice his life to gain anyone's attention, whereas before this, no one had ever been willing to listen to him or his grandmother.

There is another detail I find particularly ironic: when Blue Hair stands with De Zai, he says, "You can't do it alone; only this way can you help everyone." He was right. De Zai spent all night struggling at the service center and couldn't even get the subscription canceled, but once he collapsed, the whole TV station was brought down with him. 1 person couldn't cancel a subscription, yet a human life meant no one would ever have to cancel one again. This ending is as absurd as a joke, but when you think about it, it's not funny at all.

The most tender part of this film is the story of De Zai and his grandmother: a grandson who wants to make films, and a grandmother who wants to see her grandson's work on TV. Neither of them spoke their true feelings; one thought the other was wasting money, while the other paid an extra subscription fee just to support that dream. By the time De Zai finally understood, what his grandmother held in her hands was already a subscription cancellation notice.

Often, there is a similar inexplicable process between us and our families, where the words we want to say are only spoken when it's almost too late. I think what this film wants to say isn't just about how hard it is to cancel a subscription; it asks whether, in the face of cold rules, we still remember that behind the counter, on the other end of the line, and across the screen, there is always a living, breathing person. Grandma subscribes to the service just to watch her grandson's movie on TV; Xiao Mei returns the toy to Fatty, just wanting to be a person again. These wishes are so small, so small they shouldn't have to cost anything at all. Turning off a switch is easy; the hard part is remembering you're human during the days you're treated like a switch.
