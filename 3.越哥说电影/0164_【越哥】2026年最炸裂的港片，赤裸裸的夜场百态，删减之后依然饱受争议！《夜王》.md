"King of the Night" is a 2026 film directed by Ng Wai-lun. This film grossed 113 million HKD in Hong Kong, ranking in the top 3 in local box office history. Interestingly, the top 3 are all starring Dayo Wong: "A Guilty Conscience," "The Last Dance," and this "King of the Night." For 1 actor to sweep the top 3 of the local box office is unprecedented in decades of Hong Kong cinema; it also set records for overseas performance and was later released in Guangdong and Guangxi.

Honestly, my expectations were not high before watching it. Nightclubs, mob bosses, and scams—put these words together, and it looks like a retread of 1990s Hong Kong cinema. After watching, I feel it’s a very well-told commercial film; it’s packed with jokes, has a fast pace, and the final scheme is incredibly satisfying. But what really made me remember it were those unremarkable little details. Take Brother Huan and his ex-wife; they were married for 10 years, and the fortune teller said they could share hardship but not prosperity—I pondered that line for a long time. The night they won 420,000 in Macau, they had already reconciled, but the moment Brother Huan mentioned wanting to stay at Dongri, everything went back to square one. The vase was smashed, and Brother Huan stepped barefoot over the broken glass. Before leaving, he crouched down to push the shards aside, afraid she might cut her feet when she went out. This action had no dialogue, and the director didn't even use a close-up, but I feel the deepest emotion in the whole film is hidden in that 1 moment. Later, Prince Feng turned on them, and Wei signed for 80 million as a guarantor; even after selling her car and house, she only scraped together 10 million. It was also at this moment that the manager finally told the truth: all these years, Huan had been secretly supporting her behind the scenes. With nowhere else to go, Wei moved into the house; the bedroom was exactly as it had been when she left, with not a single thing moved. Many romance films rely on long monologues to convey subtleties, but this one says it all within a single room. On the rooftop, Sister Wei says she’s never been this afraid in her life, and Brother Huan replies, "Why be afraid when I'm with you?" He turns around and bets his entire house on the line. 10 years ago, they couldn't endure prosperity; 10 years later, they braved adversity together—perhaps that fortune-teller was right all along.

Casting Dayo Wong as Brother Huan was the perfect choice; his trademark sharp tongue but soft heart fits a nightclub veteran perfectly. In the first half, he teaches the hostesses how to conduct business, delivering one punchline after another. When Kui-fang claims she’s 19 and a half, rounding up to 20, the earpiece malfunctions; he saves the scene with a quick, "I heard you." These small-time characters aren't just filler; everyone has a role in the scheme, even Kou's betrayal was planned from the start. The layers of twists are incredibly satisfying, and the scene that left the deepest impression was Brother Huan kneeling. When the fake Vietnamese investment was exposed by Prince Feng, he knelt without a second thought; at that moment, even I thought they had lost. For a man who spent half his life in the nightclubs to set aside his pride so easily, all to help Sister Wei get back at them—and then having her curse out the Prince—was another kind of thrill; this seamless cooperation is only found in those who have been a couple for 10 years.

The conspirators include Mr. Yao, who holds a grudge against Mr. Zhou, A4 who embezzled 20 million, plus Ma Sir and Chen Dazhuang; each has their own leverage and agenda. Prince Feng bets everything on his final move, shorting 100 million in stocks, but what he really loses is his own arrogance. On another level, Dongri Nightclub represents the "underworld" of that generation: the madams lead the hostesses, the managers lead the madams, all based on personal loyalty; meanwhile, the Muse Group follows a different set of rules, where Prince Feng can bankrupt someone with a word and threaten to claim any street he sleeps on. Brother Huan’s group wins in the end, making over 100 million and seemingly getting their revenge, but the film doesn't stop there; the club still closes, they are still forced out, and they have to open a private club elsewhere—I love this approach, as it doesn't pretend the "underworld" can truly defeat capital. Winning a game is easy, but holding onto a place is too hard; that era is gone and it's never coming back. The director simply let this group exit with dignity; such clarity is far rarer than a happy ending.

Then there is the character Mimi; she didn't have much screen time, but she was the most moving to me. She had always liked Brother Huan, and after the tarot cards pushed him to go back to Wei, only when she secretly dropped an earring behind his bed did she say that many earrings had been left there. Dropping 1 earring might be an accident, but dropping many means wanting to leave something behind time and time again. The moment she raised her glass from afar said more than any confession ever could.

What is even more poignant is that, in my opinion, its box office success relied on more than just Dayo Wong's appeal; it speaks to the plight of many ordinary people. When we were young, we thought that having money would fix everything, but when life finally got easier, people drifted apart; it was in those down years that we truly saw who would walk barefoot over broken glass for us, who would keep our room just as it was, and who would bet their own home just to stand by us. As the film says at the very end, as long as people need entertainment, they will still be here. The green hills remain, and the waters flow on. I think this is also what this generation of Hong Kong filmmakers wants to say to the audience: the place may be gone, the sign may be changed, but as long as we are still here.
